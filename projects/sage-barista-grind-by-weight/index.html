zigbrew-scales: Grind by weight, not by time

I cut my dosing ring in half and put a load cell between the halves. It weighs the portafilter while the grinder fills it and stops the grinder at 18 g, instead of leaving it to the Sage's timer.

Grinds by weight, not time.

This is part two. Part one put two Zigbee relays inside my Sage Barista Express. One of them, zigbrew_grind, can press the grinder's cradle switch for half a second, and that's all this post needs.

The GRIND AMOUNT dial on the Barista Express sets a time, not a weight, and the grams you get in that time drift. Fresh beans grind faster than stale ones, finer settings are slower, and a nearly empty hopper feeds the burrs differently from a full one. A dose that's 18 g on Monday can be 17 g by Friday.

I wanted to pick a dose in grams and have the morning grind land within 0.2 g of it, without standing there watching a scale. So far the first two morning doses landed on 18.0 g and 18.1 g. The ten-dose test is still to do.

How it works

On the Barista Express you start a grind by pushing the portafilter into the cradle, and a second push stops it early. The relay from part one can already make that push, so the scale only has to weigh the grounds as they land and send one more push at the right moment.

I could have used a Bluetooth coffee scale and a Home Assistant automation. But then every reading goes through a Bluetooth proxy, Home Assistant, Zigbee2MQTT and the relay, which takes a third to two thirds of a second, and never the same amount twice. At 1.6 g a second, that wobble alone is most of a gram. So I built my own scale, which decides when to stop by itself, 84 times a second, and talks straight to the relay over a Zigbee binding, a direct device-to-device link with no hub in the middle.

The safety net is the machine's own timer. GRIND AMOUNT is turned up so the timed dose always runs a little past the heaviest target. If the scale, the network or the relay drops out, the grind still ends on the timer, a gram or two heavy. The scale only sends short presses and never holds a switch on, so it can't leave the grinder running.

Where the scale goes

The hard part was where to put a scale on a machine that holds the portafilter by its ears, on two hooks, right under the grinder outlet with no room to spare. Direction A was a cup scale shaped like a portafilter, hanging in the cradle on its own, tipped into the real portafilter afterwards. It got as far as a fit test. Direction B, the one I built, weighs straight into the portafilter.

I already use a dosing ring, which clips onto the portafilter's ears and hangs on the cradle's hooks. B is that ring, cut in two, with a load cell as the only thing joining the halves. The bottom half grips the portafilter exactly as the ring does today, so everything below the cut, portafilter and coffee, hangs from the cell.

B won because there's no cup to tip and no transfer step, so nothing changes in the morning. Balance comes free too: the portafilter's weight reaches the top ring at the nose, in front of the hooks, so the ring tips back against the machine's lip the way the real dosing ring does with a handle on it. Direction A's cup scale had its weight behind the hooks and drooped.

The cost is a 1 kg load cell instead of 100 g, because it has to carry the 492.5 g portafilter before any coffee goes in. Ten times the range means a tenth of the resolution, so each reading is noisy to ±0.13 g instead of ±0.02 g. And there's no wire: the ring goes in and out of the cradle with the portafilter every dose, so it runs on a battery.

The scale is 5 V USB and a small battery, and it never touches the machine's wiring; the relay and its mains tap are from part one. The real risk isn't electrical: a press on an idle grinder starts it, so a press at the wrong moment starts a grind nobody asked for. The firmware only presses after it's been armed, and it counts its presses.

Split the ring

Nothing that's weighed may touch anything that isn't, so the two halves sit 2.5 mm apart everywhere. A Z-shaped labyrinth in the wall stops grounds climbing out through the gap, and nothing narrows the bore: it's tamper-sized all the way through, so I tamp through the ring like today. The load cell keeps a millimetre clear of its box all round.

The CAD refuses to render if any of that is violated. By the end there were 24 touch checks, each an OpenSCAD intersection that has to come back empty, with a deliberate wrong placement to prove it trips. The useful ones are the assembly checks: can the load cell get into the box, the sleeve come up from below, the lid go on. The first draft passed every clearance check and could not be put together.

Wiring and calibration

Four wires from the cell to the HX711's screw terminal, four from the HX711 to the XIAO: data, clock, 3.3 V and ground. The clock pin is D2 because it's a low-power pin the C6 can hold high through deep sleep, which powers the HX711 down on battery. A printed jig holds both boards at exactly their spacing on the carrier, so the wires come out the right length and the wired pair lifts straight across.

I found one wiring mistake in a photo before powering up: red was on the HX711's VIO pad, one along from VIN. At first power-up it read 84 times a second, so the RATE switch really was on H.

Calibration came out at 1,074.8 counts per gram. With the coins left on for a minute the reading stayed flat with no drift, but every single reading was noisy to ±0.13 g.

Simulation

The dose logic is a pure C++ header shared by the firmware and a desktop simulator. The simulator runs it against a model grinder (start delay, flow rate, stop latency with jitter, spin-down, grounds in the chute, motor vibration, the GRIND AMOUNT timer) and throws scenarios at it: a thousand doses, the timer ending the dose right at the stop point, 20% of commands lost, a slow link, the cup put on and taken off, a weight dropped on an idle scale. Each runs at four noise levels, the last a recorded minute of the real ring replayed from a random point, because real noise has spikes and wander that Gaussian noise doesn't.

It found five bugs in the first controller before any hardware existed. The nastiest was a stop press landing just after the timer had already ended the dose. On an idle grinder a press is a start, so it quietly ground a second dose, and nothing noticed. The most surprising was the Arduino Zigbee library, which silently refuses to send an On unless it made the binding, so the firmware builds the command itself.

The dose logic now:

  1. Arm, from Home Assistant or the console, and tare over 0.4 s with the empty portafilter hanging. It refuses if the scale isn't calibrated. The tare takes out overnight creep, which is 0.02 g in ten minutes on this cell.
  2. Press once. If no grounds arrive within 4 s, give up and never press again. The machine was off or the hopper empty, and a second press would start it with nobody watching.
  3. Watch the 300 ms trend, not the reading, and press to stop at target − lead. There used to be an "is it still running?" check first. On the ring's noise it came in just under its own bar about once in two hundred doses, held off, and the grinder ran on to the timer: 23 to 34 g in an 18 g basket. I was overcomplicating it, so now it just presses.
  4. Keep watching. Grounds pouring at grinding speed 1.5 s after a press means it's still running, so press again, up to three times. That covers a lost command and a press that landed just after the timer and restarted it.
  5. Settle for 1.2 s, report, and learn. lead ← lead + 0.3 × (final − target), but only from doses it stopped itself with no rescue presses. The final weight and the new lead go to Home Assistant.

First grinds

The assembled ring went into the cradle with the portafilter clipped on, still on the laptop's USB. The boring checks all passed: the sleeve hangs free (every poke comes back to the same reading), re-clipping the portafilter repeats to 0.2 g, the bench calibration holds (two £1 coins read 17.49 g of 17.50), and noise is the same ±0.13 g as the bare cell. Then I bound the trigger endpoint to zigbrew_grind in Zigbee2MQTT and ran a real grind.

The first try pressed once, saw nothing for four seconds and gave up: the machine was off, and the relay can't start a grinder in standby. One press, no second, as designed. Machine on, armed for 18.0 g with a guessed lead of 1.0 g:

2.99 s is too close to 3 s to be chance. The scale asks the relay to confirm each command and sends it again if no confirmation comes back within 3 s (that comes from the ZBOSS library under the Arduino core's Zigbee stack). The relay had obeyed the stop, but its confirmation got lost, so the scale sent the stop again. On this relay every On is a press, so the repeat started the grinder. The relay should have spotted the repeat by its counter and ignored it; why its duplicate table missed it I still don't know. It doesn't matter for the fix: a press that is never resent can't be duplicated.

So the press now asks for no acknowledgement: the sketch builds the three-byte On frame itself and hands it to the stack with the ack flag off, so it goes out once. A press that is still lost is simply missing, which the dose logic already handles: a lost start gets no grounds and gives up, a lost stop gets a rescue press 1.5 s later. The resend had also been undoing rescues: lose the stop's first copy, the rescue stops the grinder at 1.5 s, then the 3 s resend starts it again.

The second grind, with the ring emptied, was clean: two presses, two pulses. So 0.35 g arrives after the stop press, not the 1.0 g I'd guessed for the lead. The scale learned 30% of the error on its own, and I set it to 0.35 from the console to save a few doses.

Morning automation

Part one's morning ends in one script that presses POWER, waits, and presses GRIND, each once a day. Only the GRIND press changed. If grind-by-weight is on and the scale has been heard from recently, the script arms the scale instead and records the dose. A silent scale gets the old timed press, so a dead scale still makes coffee. If the armed scale's start press never shows up on the relay, the script notifies and stops. It never presses on the scale's behalf: fail to no coffee, never to a double press.

On the first morning, a Sunday, I went to the kitchen before showering and nothing happened: the coffee was waiting for my shower, and the gym-morning trigger only runs on weekdays. So now the first motion downstairs starts it too.

So that Sunday I ran the script by hand, with the scale on a USB wall plug and the 0.35 g lead. It ground 18.0 g on an 18.0 g target in two presses, "stopped by weight", the first dose armed by Home Assistant.

Home Assistant also keeps a Coffee scale dashboard. Every dose shows up there with how many presses it took: two is normal, one is "no grounds", three or more is a rescue, and the logbook says why.

Battery and sleep

The bench build was a Zigbee router, a device that stays awake and passes messages on for others, drawing 30 to 80 mA. On a 250 mAh cell that's an afternoon. So on battery the ring is an end device, one that's allowed to sleep. Every two minutes it wakes, rejoins from what it saved in flash, reports every reading and setting, listens for a couple of seconds, and sleeps. Home Assistant can't write to a sleeping scale, so it keeps the wanted values and sends whatever differs while the scale is awake; if a grind is wanted, the check-in automation arms it. One dose per wake; a late or repeated arm is ignored. So a grind can start up to two minutes after it's asked for.

It worked first time next to the laptop upstairs, rejoining in 0.3 s. Downstairs in the cradle it went quiet. With the laptop on the kitchen counter, I found two problems.

The first was a loop in the Zigbee library. When a join times out, the Arduino core helpfully resets the saved channel list to all sixteen channels. The next 8 s join can't finish a full scan, times out, resets again, forever. One slow rejoin after the move started it. The fix: pin channel 24 before every join, and give a join after a failure 20 s instead of 8.

The second was a steady 5.5 s rejoin from the cradle. The stack picks the best-heard router whose beacon says it takes end devices, sends it a rejoin request, and waits five seconds for an answer before trying the next. Something near the machine was advertising room for children and never answering. I guessed the two relays inside the machine. I was wrong.

With no sniffer to hand, I made the firmware log what each rejoin hears. If the router it tried first never answers, the scale ignores that router from then on. The first wake after flashing logged "slow rejoin: scan at 296 ms, parent answered at 5451 ms; hiding 0x1ad5 (tried first, never answered)."

0x1ad5 is the office mmWave presence sensor, a Tuya TS0601. It's a router, its beacon offers to take end devices, it was heard best, and it never answers a rejoin. The relays inside the machine both offer too, but the scale barely hears them through the metal case, at −80 and −95 dBm, so they were never first. From the next wake: rejoin 0.5 s, awake 2.5 s, against 5.5 and 7.5 before.

Battery life is still an estimate, because the current hasn't been measured. At an assumed 80 mA awake, 2.5 s every two minutes is about 37 mAh a day, which is a week on this cell; every five minutes would be two. Battery level needs two resistors from BAT to a spare pin that aren't there yet, so for now it charges on a schedule, not on a number.

What's next

Revisit the form factor and see if it can be made better. An e-ink display on the ring showing the target and the weight the last grind reached. E-ink holds its image with no power, so it suits a scale that sleeps. And two buttons beside it to adjust the target.
